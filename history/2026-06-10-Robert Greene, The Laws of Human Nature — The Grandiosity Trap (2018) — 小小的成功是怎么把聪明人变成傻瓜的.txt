The Grandiosity Trap

In the summer of 1984, Michael Eisner was one of the most admired executives in Hollywood. As president of Paramount Pictures, he had overseen a run of hits — Raiders of the Lost Ark, Beverly Hills Cop, Flashdance — that most studios would trade a decade for. He was disciplined, creatively sharp, and genuinely good at his job. Then he became CEO of Disney. And slowly, almost invisibly, something broke.

By the mid-1990s, Eisner had stopped listening. He dismissed concerns from colleagues he once respected. He greenlit vanity projects. He managed by fear rather than curiosity. The man who had built his reputation on great taste began making decision after decision that insiders found baffling. What happened? He hadn't lost his IQ. He'd lost something subtler and more dangerous: his grip on reality.

Robert Greene calls this the Law of Grandiosity. In The Laws of Human Nature, he argues that humans have a deep, hardwired need to think highly of themselves — which is not always a bad thing. Healthy self-regard fuels confidence and persistence. But when success arrives, this need warps into something far more treacherous. We stop accurately measuring the gap between how good we think we are and how good we actually are. And the more real our success was, the more invisible the distortion becomes.

Here is the mechanism Greene identifies: success creates a narrative, and the brain — which hates randomness and loves stories — begins rewriting history. The lucky timing becomes foresight. The colleague who saved the deal becomes a minor footnote. The years of grinding preparation fade into the background of our natural genius. Psychologists call this the self-serving attribution bias: we assign our wins to ability and our losses to circumstance. Eisner didn't think he was becoming arrogant. He thought he was simply finally working at the level he had always deserved.

This is what makes the Grandiosity Trap so cruel: it is most convincing precisely when it is most active. The distorted self-image doesn't feel like delusion. It feels like clarity. You've done this before. You know how it works. Why listen to people who haven't?

Neuroscientist Michael Gazzaniga has spent decades studying the brain's 'interpreter' — the left hemisphere system that constantly constructs coherent explanations for our behavior, even when those explanations are fabricated. We don't just remember our past; we curate it, editing out the disconfirming evidence, unconsciously smoothing the story into one where we were the hero all along. The more successful you become, the more material the interpreter has to work with, and the more persuasive the resulting fiction.

Greene points to a pattern across history that is almost distressingly predictable. The conqueror who wins brilliantly at 30 and catastrophically overextends at 50. The founder who builds something remarkable through radical openness, then spends the next decade slowly eliminating anyone who disagrees with them. Napoleon's invasion of Russia. Eisner's later Disney years. The CEO who can no longer hear bad news because every filter in the organization has learned that bad news is career-ending. In each case, the person was not corrupted by failure. They were corrupted by success.

What does this look like at a smaller, more personal scale? It looks like the manager who got promoted for being decisive and now can't distinguish decisiveness from stubbornness. It looks like the entrepreneur who had one great exit and now approaches every problem as though they have the solution before hearing the question. It looks like the teacher, the parent, the advisor who stopped asking whether their mental model of the situation might be wrong, because experience has made them confident — and confidence, over time, has quietly become closure.

The antidote Greene offers is not false humility. It is what he calls 'realistic self-assessment' — a deliberate, almost scientific practice of measuring your self-opinion against concrete feedback from the world. Not the curated feedback from people who need something from you, but the raw, uncomfortable kind: Are the results actually there? What do the people closest to the work think, when they're being honest? What patterns do you keep attributing to external circumstances that might, in fact, be you?

The Stoics had a practice worth stealing here. Marcus Aurelius — who was an emperor, a man with every structural reason to grow grandiose — returned again and again in his private journals to a single discipline: see yourself as others see you. Not to be crushed by their opinion, but to use it as a corrective lens. The goal was not self-deprecation but calibration.

Greene's sharpest observation may be this: the most dangerous form of grandiosity is not the loud, boastful kind. That version is easy to spot and easy to dismiss. The dangerous kind is quiet and sincere. It wears the face of experience. It sounds like wisdom. It says: I have been through enough to know. And by the time you realize it isn't wisdom — it's a closed door — the costs have already compounded.

Success is not inherently corrupting. But success without a feedback mechanism is. The goal is not to distrust your achievements. It is to build, deliberately and stubbornly, the structures that keep your self-image tethered to reality: advisors who are paid to disagree with you, metrics that don't care how you feel, a practice of actively seeking out the evidence that challenges your narrative rather than waiting for the world to force it on you.

The same competence that got you here will not protect you from this. In fact, it makes you more vulnerable. That's the trap.

🎬 Today's action: Think of one recent decision you made with high confidence. Write down, in one sentence, what evidence would have to exist for that decision to be wrong — and then go look for it.
📌 Takeaway: Success doesn't corrupt your intelligence; it corrupts your feedback loop. Protect the loop and the intelligence stays useful.
💡 Extension: Arthur C. Brooks in From Strength to Strength has a similar observation — the very strengths that build a career in its first half become the blind spots that stall it in the second. Greene and Brooks are describing the same trap from different angles.
