The Moment You Finally Get What You Wanted

You spent two years pursuing it. A relationship, a role, a goal that felt like the answer. You restructured your week around it. And then, one day, you had it.

For about three weeks, it felt exactly as good as you imagined.

Then came the flatness. A quiet, inexplicable deflation. You looked at the thing you had worked so hard for and felt not happiness, but something closer to mild confusion. Where did the feeling go?

Nothing is wrong with you. You have run into one of the most consistent mechanisms in human psychology, one Robert Greene maps in The Art of Seduction (2001): the disenchantment trap.

## Why Desire Needs Distance

Greene's argument is not really about romance. It is about the architecture of desire itself. Desire, he argues, is not a response to something's value. It is a response to incompleteness, tension, and the not-yet. The moment those conditions disappear — the moment you fully possess the thing — desire has nothing left to feed on.

This maps onto what psychologist Richard Solomon called the opponent-process theory of motivation. Every intense emotional state generates an opposing state of roughly equal force. The initial high is followed by a counterforce pulling in the opposite direction. The first time feels extraordinary. The tenth time feels ordinary. The pull of the unattained is not immaturity. It is how wanting structurally works.

## The Marquise Who Understood Endings

Greene illustrates this through a character from Choderlos de Laclos's Dangerous Liaisons — the Marquise de Merteuil, a practiced social strategist. When she decides an affair has run its course, she does not end it through rejection or distance, which would only intensify attachment. Instead she does something counterintuitive: she floods her lover with total presence.

She removes all mystery. She becomes completely available. She eliminates the gaps, the tension, the sense that something in her remains just beyond reach. Her lover, Chevalier de Belleroche, gradually loses interest. Without incompleteness, there is nothing for desire to work against. He ends the relationship himself, believing it was his own decision.

This is Greene's most unsettling insight: what we call passion is not really about the object itself. It is about what the object represents while it is still unfinished. A goal carrying the charge of possibility feels alive. The same goal, fully possessed and thoroughly understood, starts to feel like furniture.

## The Two Traps

This mechanism creates two specific traps in ordinary life.

The first is the pursuit trap. We organize enormous energy around attaining something not because that thing will satisfy us, but because the pursuit itself is generating meaning. Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer at Harvard found in their Progress Principle research that forward momentum — not arrival — is the primary driver of daily well-being. We are not built for destinations. We are built for movement toward them.

The second is what Tal Ben-Shahar called the arrival fallacy. After studying people who reach major goals — Olympic athletes after winning gold, executives landing dream roles — he found the pattern nearly universal: a brief peak, followed by a drop that often lands below the pre-achievement baseline. We mistook the energy of pursuit for a promise about what arrival would actually feel like.

## What Stays Alive

Greene is not being cynical. He is pointing toward a more honest relationship with motivation. The pursuits that remain alive are those never fully resolved — that always contain a next horizon, a new layer, something that keeps incompleteness intact.

The best long-term relationships are not where partners fully know each other. They are where partners keep generating genuine curiosity. The best careers are not where mastery is secured. They are where the practitioner keeps finding new edges of difficulty.

Desire does not die because you failed. It dies because you finished.

🎬 Today's action: Identify one thing you are currently pursuing that feels urgent and alive. Ask honestly: if I had it fully tomorrow, what would give me a reason to stay curious about it six months from now? If you cannot answer that, the pursuit may be more about the chase than the thing itself — and that is worth knowing before you arrive.

📌 Takeaway: The flatness after getting what you wanted is not ingratitude or restlessness. It is the opponent-process mechanism doing exactly what it was designed to do. The antidote is not to want less — it is to build goals that preserve incompleteness: relationships, projects, and pursuits with a horizon that keeps moving.

💡 Extension: Tal Ben-Shahar in Happier (2007) argues directly for the same idea — the arrival fallacy is one of the most common sources of modern unhappiness, and the solution lies in finding meaning in the journey rather than treating the destination as the final answer.
