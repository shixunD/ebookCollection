The Friend Who Wants You to Fail

Here is a question most people refuse to answer honestly: When someone close to you — a friend, a colleague, a peer in your field — announces a big win, what is the very first thing you feel?

Not what you say. Not the congratulatory emoji you type. The first flicker, before the social mask goes on.

If there is a split second of something cold — a small tightening in the chest, a quiet voice saying why them and not me — you are not broken. You are human. And you are experiencing one of the most powerful forces Robert Greene describes in The Laws of Human Nature (2018): the Law of Envy.

## The Emotion That Hides in Plain Sight

Greene's central claim about envy is deceptively simple: it is the one emotion we almost never recognize in ourselves. Anger, we feel and own. Sadness, we sit with. But envy? The moment it stirs, the brain immediately converts it into something else — righteous indignation, a critique of the other person's luck, concern for fairness. 'I'm not envious,' the internal narrator insists, 'I just think they didn't really earn it.'

This is not a moral failure. It is a psychological reflex. Envy requires admitting inferiority in something we care about. That admission is intolerable, so the mind outsources the emotion and relabels it. The poison stays in the bloodstream, but the label on the bottle is changed.

The research supports Greene's observation. Studies in social psychology — including work by Richard Smith at the University of Kentucky — show that schadenfreude, or pleasure at another's misfortune, spikes most intensely not toward strangers or enemies, but toward people who are similar to us and in the same domain. Your colleague gets promoted; your neighbor buys a better car; your old classmate publishes a book. Proximity and relevance are the triggers, not distance.

## The Fatal Friend

Greene illustrates this through the story of Jane Williams and Mary Shelley. In the 1820s, Jane became close to Mary — author of Frankenstein, wife of the poet Percy Shelley — through shared circumstances and genuine affection. But as the friendship deepened, something darker grew alongside it. Mary had what Jane coveted: a brilliant reputation, illustrious connections, a husband who adored her intellectually, a life that felt larger.

Jane did not think: I am envious. She thought: Mary is not as great as people say. She is difficult. She does not deserve Percy's devotion. She constructed a narrative — piece by piece, quietly, with sincere conviction — that justified the sabotage she eventually carried out. She seduced Percy Shelley, deepened the rift in their marriage, and after Percy died, spread stories in London that Mary had never truly loved him.

The cruelty was not premeditated. It was rationalized, step by step, from within a friendship.

This is Greene's most uncomfortable insight: envy does not come from enemies. It comes from the people closest to us, precisely because closeness amplifies the comparison. Fatal friends — Greene's term — are often drawn to us because of the envy. The friendship becomes a way to stay near what they want, and later, a vehicle for quiet destruction.

## What This Means for You

There are two directions to face here.

First, looking outward: Greene advises learning to read the early signals. The effusive praise that feels slightly off-key. The helpful advice that nudges you toward a mistake. The strange absence when something good happens to you. These are not proof of malice, but they are worth noticing. Schopenhauer had a practical test: share good news with someone and watch the first millisecond of their face, before the smile arrives. The flash of disappointment — what psychologists now call a microexpression — tells you more than everything that follows.

Second, and more importantly, looking inward. The next time you feel that quiet tightening when a peer succeeds, do not rush past it. Sit with it for one moment. Greene calls the antidote Mitfreude — philosopher Nietzsche's term for joying with another, the genuine capacity to feel someone else's happiness as your own. It is rare. It is difficult. And it is, Greene argues, the highest expression of social intelligence. Not because it makes you a saint, but because it frees you from the exhausting loop of comparison that corrodes your own sense of possibility.

The person you envy is not the obstacle to your growth. The envy itself is.

🎬Today's action: The next time you hear good news from a peer, pause before responding. Notice the first thing you feel — honestly. Then, instead of performing congratulation, try to actually imagine their specific joy in that moment. Name it out loud to yourself: 'She has been working on this for three years.' That act of specificity is where Mitfreude begins.

📌Takeaway: Envy is not what you feel toward enemies. It is what you feel toward people just like you — and it almost always disguises itself as something more acceptable. Learning to catch it early, in yourself and others, is one of the most underrated skills in human relationships.

💡 Extension: Richard Smith in Envy: Theory and Research (2008) offers converging evidence from social psychology: envy is most acute when the comparison is with someone in our comparison group — same age, same field, same aspirations. His findings confirm Greene's intuition that proximity, not distance, is what turns admiration into poison.
